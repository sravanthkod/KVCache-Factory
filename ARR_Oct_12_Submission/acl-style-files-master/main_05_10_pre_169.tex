\begin{abstract}
Key-Value (KV) cache consumption is a critical bottleneck for efficient LLM inference, especially on long-context workloads. Token eviction methods such as SnapKV, H2O, AdaKV and L2Norm reduce this footprint, but they give every transformer layer the same cache budget, although layers differ in which tokens they need. We propose MOSAIC, a multi-objective neural architecture search approach that discovers per-layer KV-cache budget allocations tailored to a given eviction method. MOSAIC treats average cache budget and task score as competing objectives in a four-stage pipeline: a single unconstrained search maps the trade-off between budget and score, its best layer-wise allocation is transferred to any target budget, and a budget-constrained search can refine it further. On Llama-3-8B-Instruct, MOSAIC improves over uniform allocation for all four eviction methods, with gains of up to +48 points on RULER and up to +11.9 points on LongBench (LCC). One search serves many budgets: the winner transfers to other budgets without further search, including budgets far below the one at which it was found. Configurations are selected and evaluated on the full benchmark data. The learned allocations consistently favour a small set of middle layers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Long-context inference with large language models (LLMs) is limited by the memory of the key-value (KV) cache, which grows linearly with sequence length and batch size: a 30B-parameter model with batch size 128 and sequence length 1024 already needs 180\,GB of KV cache \citep{zhang2023h2o}. Token eviction methods like H2O \citep{zhang2023h2o}, SnapKV \citep{li2024snapkv} and L2Norm \citep{devoto2024l2norm} address this by selectively dropping cache entries based on token importance, and AdaKV \citep{feng2025adakv} builds on them by distributing a layer's budget adaptively across its attention heads. However, these methods apply the same memory budget to every transformer layer. Layer-wise allocation schemes exist, but they either fix the shape of the allocation in advance \citep{cai2025pyramidkv}, derive it from attention statistics \citep{qin2025cake}, or optimize layer budgets one group at a time at one budget and rescale the result to others \citep{yu2025evolkv}. This overlooks a critical insight: different layers prioritize tokens differently. In our searches, a few middle layers consistently receive more than their uniform share, so a uniform allocation spends cache on layers that need little while starving the ones that need more.

This paper introduces MOSAIC, a method that automatically optimizes per-layer KV-cache budgets under a target memory constraint using multi-objective neural architecture search. Rather than hand-tuning layer-specific heuristics or optimizing layers group by group, MOSAIC searches the per-layer budget space jointly, one budget per decoder layer (32 for Llama-3-8B), using Bayesian optimization guided by a surrogate MLP (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). MOSAIC is to KV-cache allocation what static quantization is to model quantization: just as static quantization fixes scales and zero points on a calibration set, MOSAIC fixes each layer's budget offline on calibration data, so inference adds no per-prompt allocation step. Methods such as CAKE and Ada-KV instead adapt the budget to each prompt at inference time, as dynamic quantization computes its ranges at run time. We evaluate MOSAIC across four eviction methods (SnapKV, H2O, AdaKV, L2Norm) and two benchmarks: LongBench (4 task categories) and RULER (11 in-context retrieval subtasks). On RULER, per-layer allocation yields large gains over uniform (+2.3 to +47.9 points), concentrated on the hardest needle-retrieval subtasks. On LongBench, MOSAIC improves every Code setting, with dataset-level gains of up to +11.9 points on LCC (L2Norm), +3.5 on LCC for SnapKV, and +3.0 on MultiFieldQA-en (H2O) and Qasper (AdaKV) (Section~\ref{sec:which-stage}).

\textbf{MOSAIC versus EvolKV.}\label{sec:intro-evolkv} The closest prior work is EvolKV \citep{yu2025evolkv}, which also searches per-layer budgets against downstream task score, using the evolutionary strategy CMA-ES. It optimizes the budgets block by block (groups of 8 layers, earlier groups frozen) at one fixed budget, scores candidates on 30 calibration samples, and extrapolates the result to other budgets. MOSAIC is a complete allocation-search workflow that differs at each step (Appendix~\ref{app:evolkv-glance}): (i) it searches all per-layer budgets jointly, so each layer's budget is chosen together with the others rather than after earlier groups are frozen; (ii) one unconstrained search, which treats the average budget as a second objective instead of fixing it, explores the budget--score trade-off, so the winning shape is found at the budget where it works best rather than one fixed in advance; (iii) it rescales that shape to every budget, as EvolKV does, and optionally refines it per budget; and (iv) it selects the final configuration by its score on the full evaluation data rather than on a small calibration subset, because on LongBench small subsets can rank configurations differently from the full data and inflate scores: EvolKV's 30-sample scores exceed its full-data scores by 6.7--9.8 points. Searching once, MOSAIC's winner beats EvolKV on Code at every budget ($+1.3$ to $+2.1$) and matches it on Single-Doc QA; per budget, with Stage 4 truncated to EvolKV's compute, it wins 5 of 6 cells (Section~\ref{sec:evolkv}).

Our contributions are: (i) MOSAIC, a four-stage workflow (joint per-layer search, budget--score exploration, winner rescaling, optional per-budget refinement) whose single search yields a winner that transfers across budgets without further search; (ii) an evaluation across four eviction methods and two benchmarks, where MOSAIC improves over uniform allocation for every method, with gains of up to +48 points on RULER; and (iii) an analysis of the learned allocations, which favour a small set of middle layers.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Token eviction.} Eviction methods reduce KV-cache memory by retaining only the tokens judged important. H2O \citep{zhang2023h2o} keeps ``heavy hitters'', tokens with high accumulated attention, together with a window of recent tokens. SnapKV \citep{li2024snapkv} selects, for each attention head, the prompt positions that receive the most attention from an observation window at the end of the prompt. L2Norm \citep{devoto2024l2norm} keeps the tokens whose keys have the lowest $L_2$ norm, which correlates with high attention, without computing attention scores. KeyDiff \citep{park2025keydiff} also avoids attention scores, keeping keys that are geometrically distinctive from the others, which makes it compatible with FlashAttention. QUOKA \citep{jones2026quoka} selects key-value pairs for prefill from the keys aligned with a subset of representative queries. These methods decide which tokens to keep; how much cache each layer should receive is a separate question, which MOSAIC addresses.

\textbf{Static and dynamic budget allocation.} A second line of work allocates a fixed total budget non-uniformly instead of changing how tokens are scored. Dynamic methods recompute the allocation for every prompt, as dynamic quantization computes its ranges at run time: Ada-KV \citep{feng2025adakv} allocates budget adaptively across the heads of a layer, and CAKE \citep{qin2025cake} derives per-layer budgets from layer preferences measured on each prompt's attention patterns. Static methods fix the allocation before inference: PyramidKV \citep{cai2025pyramidkv} assigns layer budgets following a hand-designed pyramidal shape, larger in lower layers and smaller in higher ones. MOSAIC is static in the sense of static quantization, calibrating the allocation offline for each model and eviction method. Closest to our work, EvolKV \citep{yu2025evolkv} treats per-layer budgets as optimization variables, partitions layers into groups, and runs evolutionary search on one group at a time against downstream task score at one target budget; its main results then extend that allocation to larger budgets by proportional rescaling, without further optimization. MOSAIC shares this rescaling step; its differences from EvolKV are summarized in Section~\ref{sec:intro-evolkv} and Appendix~\ref{app:evolkv-glance}.

\textbf{Neural architecture search.} Multi-objective NAS trades off competing goals such as accuracy and latency along a Pareto front \citep{elsken2018neural}. MOSAIC adopts this multi-objective formulation for KV-cache allocation but uses its own search procedure (Section~\ref{sec:method}): a classifier-guided optimizer built for this problem, which needs only a few hundred evaluations per search.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Given an eviction method $M$, a model with $L$ decoder layers ($L = 32$ for Llama-3-8B) and a target budget $B$ (average tokens per layer), we seek a vector $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, \ldots, b_L) \in [b_{\min}, b_{\max}]^L$ that maximizes task performance while maintaining $\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L} b_i = B$. We use a per-layer floor $b_{\min} = 64$ (Appendix~\ref{app:floor}) and cap $b_{\max} = 4096$. Each configuration is encoded as $\mathbf{x} \in [0,1]^L$. The unconstrained search is multi-objective: it minimizes the mean per-layer budget $f_1(\mathbf{b}) = \frac{1}{L}\sum_i b_i$ and maximizes the task score $f_2(\mathbf{b})$ on a calibration subsample. The budget-constrained variant (Stage 4) instead fixes $f_1 = B$ by rescaling the per-layer weights so that their mean equals $B$ exactly.

\textbf{Search step.} All stages share one classifier-guided Bayesian optimization procedure. After each evaluation, the archive is non-dominated sorted, an MLP classifier (two hidden layers of 32 units) is trained to separate the rank-1 configurations from the rest, and differential evolution maximizes the classifier's probability of rank 1 over $[0,1]^L$ to propose the next configuration, one evaluation per iteration. We call these BO proposals; stopping rules are in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\subsection{Search Procedure (MOSAIC — Four-Stage Pipeline)}

\textbf{Why four stages.} An unconstrained search returns configurations at arbitrary average budgets, so a configuration it finds cannot be compared directly with uniform allocation at a fixed budget $B$. MOSAIC therefore selects one winner shape from the search (Stages 1--2) and rescales it to each target budget (Stage 3). This already gives a configuration at every budget without further search, and the pipeline can stop here. Stage 4 is optional: it searches at a fixed budget for a configuration better than the rescaled winner (Figure~\ref{fig:method}).

\textbf{Tasks.}\label{sec:method-tasks} MOSAIC searches separately for each task and eviction method. LongBench has four English task categories, each a group of datasets: Single-Doc QA (NarrativeQA, Qasper, MultiFieldQA-en), Multi-Doc QA (HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue), Summarization (GovReport, QMSum, MultiNews) and Code (LCC, RepoBench-P); a category's score is the mean over its datasets. We search each LongBench category separately because their tasks and metrics differ (F1 for QA, ROUGE-L for summarization, edit similarity for code). RULER's 11 retrieval subtasks at 4K context share one metric (string-match accuracy), so a single search covers all of them. Stages 1--2 run once per (task, method) and Stages 3--4 once per target budget; we call each (task, method, budget) a \emph{cell}.

\textbf{Stage 1 (Unconstrained Multi-Budget NAS):} Search a discrete per-layer grid of powers of two with no budget constraint, from 64 up to 4096 on RULER and up to 1024 or 4096 on LongBench (the grid of each run is in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). The initial design has 64 points: the uniform allocation at every grid level plus Latin Hypercube samples. Guided iterations then trace out the budget--score Pareto front. Because this search must rank allocations across the whole budget range, and runs only once per (task, method), it uses a larger calibration subsample on LongBench (30\%; case study in Appendix~\ref{app:calib}); on RULER it uses 10\%, which already ranks faithfully (Appendix~\ref{app:calib}).

\textbf{Stage 2 (Winner Selection via Full-Data Eval):} Re-evaluate every configuration on the Stage 1 Pareto front (typically 10--20 configurations) on the full test data, and keep the \textbf{winner shape}: the shaped configuration with the highest full-data score. This winner becomes a universal anchor for all subsequent budget-constrained searches.



\textbf{Stage 3 (Winner Rescaling to Fixed Budgets):} Take the winner shape discovered in Stage 2 and rescale it to match fixed target budgets: B128--B1024 on LongBench and B64--B2048 on RULER (B64 only where the floor of 16 allows reallocation). Evaluate each rescaled configuration on full test data. These Stage 3 results (\textbf{Winner-Rescaled}) require no further search, and they already beat uniform in 72 of 82 (category, method, budget) cells, including shapes found at an average budget of $\sim$2400 transferred down to 128. Table~\ref{tab:transfer} summarizes the transfer; all 82 cells are in Appendix~\ref{app:transfer}.

\textbf{Stage 4 (Budget-Constrained Adaptive Search):} For each target budget $B$, Stage 4 solves $\max_{\mathbf{x} \in [0,1]^L} f_2(D_B(\mathbf{x}))$, where the decoder $D_B$ maps $\mathbf{x}$ to budgets with $\frac{1}{L}\sum_i b_i = B$ and $b_{\min} \le b_i \le b_{\max}$: it sets $b_i \propto x_i$ scaled to the total $LB$, clamps layers that cross a bound and redistributes the remainder in proportion to $x_i$, then rounds so that the total is exactly $LB$ (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). Every candidate thus meets the budget by construction, and with $f_1$ fixed at $B$ the search maximizes $f_2$ alone. Seed this search with: (i) the Stage 2 winner rescaled to budget $B$ (from Stage 3), (ii) six heuristic shapes (uniform, ascending and descending ramps, middle- and edge-heavy triangles, and an alternating low/high pattern), and (iii) 57 Latin Hypercube (LHS) seed points, all drawn inside the constrained per-layer space (continuous weights rescaled to mean $B$). Guided BO iterations then extend this pool; run lengths varied across methods and cells, up to a cap of 1000 evaluations (stopping rules in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). Stage 4 runs once for every target budget and evaluates many configurations each time, so it uses a smaller subsample (10\% on both benchmarks) to keep each per-budget search affordable. Its best candidates are re-evaluated on full data, and the best of them is reported as \textbf{NAS-refined} (candidate selection in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}; calibration fidelity in Appendix~\ref{app:calib}). For each budget we thus report uniform, the rescaled Stage 2 winner (\textbf{Winner}), and NAS-refined; \textbf{MOSAIC's final configuration is the better of Winner and NAS-refined on full data} (Section~\ref{sec:which-stage}). Stage 4 does not always improve on the Winner: of the 74 cells in which both were evaluated, NAS-refined matches or beats the Winner in 50, and the Winner remains better in 24 (Figure~\ref{fig:stages}).

\textbf{Selection and evaluation data.} The \emph{full data} of a cell are all examples of its task's datasets (150--500 per LongBench dataset, 500 per RULER subtask); the \emph{calibration subset} is a fixed random fraction of each dataset (30\% or 10\%, as above). Search uses the calibration subset; selection in Stages 2 and 4 and all reported scores use the full data. LongBench and RULER have no validation split, so configurations are selected and scored on the same examples: our results show gains on the evaluated examples, not generalization to unseen ones.

\subsection{Key Findings — LongBench}
\label{sec:which-stage}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the budget--score trade-off that the unconstrained search finds. Across all panels, 169 of 241 Stage 2 configurations lie above the uniform curve at the same average budget; the rest fall on or below it, which is why Stage 2 selects on full data. The winner lies above the uniform curve in all 16 panels where uniform runs cover its budget. SnapKV's Code winner (58.26 at average budget 430) beats uniform at every budget up to 1024 (at most 56.73), and its Multi-Doc QA winner (36.14 at 274) beats uniform-1024 (35.87) with 27\% of its cache.

\textbf{Where MOSAIC loses.} Across all evaluated cells, MOSAIC beats uniform in 71 of 75 (SnapKV and H2O: 35/38; AdaKV: 14/15; L2Norm: 22/22), including every CODE, Summarization and RULER cell. The four losses are small and all on LongBench QA: Multi-Doc QA for H2O at B128 ($-0.41$) and SnapKV at B512 ($-0.04$), and Single-Doc QA for SnapKV at B256 ($-0.74$) and AdaKV at B512 ($-0.20$). Seven wins, mostly on Summarization and Multi-Doc QA, are as small ($<+0.25$). Per-dataset results, including small dataset-level regressions, are in Appendix~\ref{app:dataset-reg}.

\subsection{Key Findings — RULER}
\label{sec:ruler}

Figure~\ref{fig:stages} (RULER column) shows the results per budget; Table~\ref{tab:ruler-summary} in Appendix~\ref{app:cells} lists MOSAIC's final configuration for each method and budget. Every RULER cell improves on uniform, for all four methods: SnapKV by $+3.1$ to $+10.4$ (93.80 vs.\ 85.67 at B1024), AdaKV by $+4.1$ to $+8.3$ from B128 to B2048, H2O by $+18.2$ to $+33.3$ at B1024--B2048 ($+3.2$ at B128, floor 64), and L2Norm by $+2.3$ to $+47.9$, all with floor 64. The largest gains are over weak uniform baselines: at B1536--B2048, H2O and L2Norm reach 86--97 with MOSAIC, about the level of uniform SnapKV (88.6--92.9) and below SnapKV with MOSAIC (98.4--98.9).

Gains concentrate on the hardest needle subtasks: for SnapKV and H2O (floor-clean cells), MOSAIC adds $+31.9$ on niah\_multikey\_3 and $+28.0$ on niah\_single\_3, versus $+0.8$, $+3.5$ and $+5.6$ on CWE, FWE and VT; for AdaKV the largest gains are also on niah\_multikey\_3 ($+49$ to $+82$ at B1024--B2048). L2Norm's uniform allocation fails most needle subtasks even at B1024, and MOSAIC recovers them (Appendix~\ref{app:ruler}).

\textbf{Caveat.} RULER B1024--B2048 for SnapKV, H2O and AdaKV used the earlier floor of 16; at B1024 the H2O configuration places 66\% of its layers there and trades CWE accuracy (98$\rightarrow$22) for needle retrieval. L2Norm used floor 64 throughout (Appendix~\ref{app:floor}).

\subsection{Shared Layer Structure}
\label{sec:shared}

The allocations share a common shape (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}). Across SnapKV and H2O cells, six middle layers (10, 14--16, 18 and 20) receive more than their uniform share in 60--76\% of the 42 allocations (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}a), whereas early (0--9) and late (21--29) layers do so in 17--55\% (median 33\% and 31\%). The 42 are not independent: 12 are rescaled Winners that repeat an anchor's shape, 5 are fixed heuristic shapes and 3 are uniform. On the 22 that come directly from search (10 anchors and 12 Stage 4 configurations), the six layers still exceed their share in 55--82\%, against medians of 34\% and 36\% for early and late layers. AdaKV shows a weaker version (Appendix~\ref{app:adakv-shapes}). Allocations are task-specific, though: SnapKV's Code winner applied to Single-Doc QA, and the reverse, falls below uniform at every budget tested (by 0.33--1.22 points). Across eviction methods on the same task they transfer better: H2O's and SnapKV's Code winners beat uniform under the other method at every budget ($+0.06$ to $+1.66$), though not the receiving method's final configuration (Appendix~\ref{app:transfer}).

\subsection{Task and Method Patterns}

Gains are largest on RULER, intermediate on Code and smallest on LongBench QA; one untested hypothesis is that they track how localized the needed evidence is. Across methods, H2O shows the largest RULER gains ($+18$ to $+33$ at B1024--B2048). AdaKV, which already allocates budget adaptively across heads within a layer, still gains from reallocation across layers: $+4.1$ to $+8.3$ on RULER and up to $+1.98$ on LongBench, comparable to SnapKV. L2Norm shows the largest LongBench gains (Code $+4.9$ to $+9.5$), but from a weak baseline: at B128 its uniform Single-Doc QA score is 10.2, against 33.0 for SnapKV, and even with MOSAIC its Code score at B1024 (44.47) stays far below uniform SnapKV (56.02). Large gains over a method's own uniform allocation do not imply strong absolute performance.

\section{Comparison with EvolKV}
\label{sec:evolkv}

We compare MOSAIC with a reproduction of EvolKV's core recipe \citep{yu2025evolkv}: CMA-ES optimizes the 32 layer budgets in 4 groups of 8, one group at a time with earlier groups frozen, against a 30-sample calibration score, and the result is rescaled to the exact target budget. We run both of its settings: a separate search at each budget (\emph{direct}) and its main protocol, one search at budget 128 whose allocation is extended to larger budgets with EvolKV's proportional completion rule and no further optimization (\emph{expanded}). We use SnapKV on Code and Single-Doc QA and re-evaluate every configuration on the full test data with the same harness as Stage 4 (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). This compares complete protocols, not optimizers: EvolKV searches and selects on 30 calibration samples per task, whereas MOSAIC selects on the full evaluation data (550 and 1{,}000 examples; Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), so part of MOSAIC's advantage may come from its larger selection set, and both are scored on examples that informed selection.

\textbf{Compute} (Appendix~\ref{app:cost}). \emph{Search once}, MOSAIC's Stages 1--2 cost more than EvolKV's budget-128 search (Single-Doc QA: 20.1 vs.\ 5.2 GPU-hours). \emph{Per budget, as run}, Stage 4 cost 84.9 GPU-hours on the 6 cells, re-evaluation included, against 53.7 for EvolKV; 51.1 at 200 evaluations per run is only a projection. \emph{Capped prefix}: truncated to EvolKV's per-cell GPU-hours (47.2 in total), NAS-refined still beats direct EvolKV in 5 of 6 cells (Code $+0.81$ to $+1.71$, a lower bound at B128; Table~\ref{tab:app-capped}). \FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}

On the evaluated examples, MOSAIC's per-layer budgets beat uniform allocation in 71 of 75 cells across four eviction methods, with large gains on RULER. One search transfers across budgets, full-data selection matters on LongBench, and the learned allocations favour a few middle layers.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Evaluation data:} configurations are selected on the same benchmark examples on which they are scored; we do not evaluate on held-out examples.
\item \textbf{Cost:} including the one-time Stages 1--2, per-budget refinement costs more than EvolKV (Section~\ref{sec:evolkv}), and AdaKV searches are slower (Appendix~\ref{app:cost}).
\item \textbf{Uneven settings:} some RULER cells used a per-layer floor of 16, AdaKV's LongBench winners were selected on calibration score, and stopping rules differed across methods (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
\item \textbf{Scope:} one model (Llama-3-8B-Instruct); allocations are searched per task; the guided search is not compared with random search at equal cost (Appendix~\ref{app:dynamics}).
\end{itemize}
