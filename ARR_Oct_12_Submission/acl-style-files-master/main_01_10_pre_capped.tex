\begin{abstract}
Key-Value (KV) cache consumption is a critical bottleneck for efficient LLM inference, especially on long-context workloads. While recent token eviction methods (SnapKV, H2O, AdaKV, L2Norm) reduce memory footprint, they apply uniform cache budgets across all transformer layers, overlooking the fact that different layers have fundamentally different token importance patterns. We propose MOSAIC, a multi-objective neural architecture search approach that discovers per-layer KV-cache budget allocations tailored to a given eviction algorithm. MOSAIC treats average cache budget and task score as competing objectives, runs a single unconstrained search to obtain a Pareto front of layer-wise allocations, selects a winner by full-data evaluation, and refines it with budget-constrained Bayesian optimization. On Llama-3-8B-Instruct with four eviction methods (SnapKV, H2O, AdaKV, L2Norm), MOSAIC beats uniform allocation in 63 of 67 evaluated cells, with gains of +2 to +48 points on RULER; the four losses are small ($\leq$0.74 points) and all on LongBench QA. A single search amortizes across budgets: the winner, rescaled with no further search, beats uniform in 68 of 78 cells. All scores are on the benchmark examples used for selection; an independent held-out evaluation remains future work. We further show that subsample-based search scores are unreliable on LongBench (the subsample's best candidate is the true best in only 17 of 48 cells), which makes full-data selection essential, and that the learned allocations consistently favor middle layers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Long-context inference with large language models (LLMs) is limited by the memory of the key-value (KV) cache, which grows linearly with sequence length and batch size: a 30B-parameter model with batch size 128 and sequence length 1024 already needs 180\,GB of KV cache \citep{zhang2023h2o}. Token eviction methods like H2O \citep{zhang2023h2o}, SnapKV \citep{li2024snapkv} and L2Norm \citep{devoto2024l2norm} address this by selectively dropping cache entries based on token importance. SnapKV reports an 8.2$\times$ memory-efficiency gain on 16K-token inputs, and L2Norm reduces the cache by 50\% on language modelling and needle-in-a-haystack tasks and by 90\% on passkey retrieval without losing accuracy. However, these methods apply the same memory budget to every transformer layer. Layer-wise allocation schemes exist, but they either fix the shape of the allocation in advance \citep{cai2025pyramidkv}, derive it from attention statistics \citep{qin2025cake}, or optimize layer budgets one group at a time at one budget and rescale the result to others \citep{yu2025evolkv}. This overlooks a critical insight: different layers prioritize tokens differently. In our searches, a few middle layers consistently receive more than their uniform share, so a uniform allocation spends cache on layers that need little while starving the ones that need more.

This paper introduces MOSAIC, a method that automatically optimizes per-layer KV-cache budgets under a target memory constraint using multi-objective neural architecture search. Rather than hand-tuning layer-specific heuristics or optimizing layers group by group, MOSAIC searches the 32-dimensional per-layer budget space jointly using Bayesian optimization guided by a surrogate MLP. We evaluate MOSAIC across four eviction methods (SnapKV, H2O, AdaKV, L2Norm) and two benchmarks: LongBench (4 task categories) and RULER (11 in-context retrieval subtasks). On RULER, where layer importance is most pronounced, per-layer optimization yields large gains (+2.3 to +47.9 points over uniform) while maintaining strict memory budgets, concentrated on the hardest needle-retrieval subtasks. On LongBench, gains are smaller: every CODE cell improves, while QA shows four small losses that we report explicitly.

\textbf{Why MOSAIC rather than EvolKV.}\label{sec:intro-evolkv} EvolKV \citep{yu2025evolkv}, the closest prior work, optimizes per-layer budgets block by block (groups of 8, earlier groups frozen) at one fixed budget on 30 calibration samples, then extrapolates. MOSAIC differs in four ways (Appendix~\ref{app:evolkv-glance}): (i) it optimizes all 32 layer budgets jointly; (ii) its single unconstrained search maps the whole budget--score trade-off, so the winner is found at its natural budget instead of a budget fixed in advance; (iii) it selects by full-data rather than calibration scores, which on LongBench misrank configurations and overstate EvolKV's own scores by 6.7--9.8 points; and (iv) it is validated across four eviction methods and two benchmarks. Searching once, MOSAIC's winner beats EvolKV on Code at every budget ($+1.3$ to $+2.1$) and matches it on Single-Doc QA, though its one-time search costs more; at matched per-budget cost, its constrained search wins 5 of 6 cells (Section~\ref{sec:evolkv}).

Our contributions are: (i) a four-stage pipeline whose single joint, multi-objective search yields a winner that transfers across budgets (the rescaled winner beats uniform in 68/78 cells); (ii) evidence that calibration-subsample scores do not reliably rank configurations on LongBench, motivating full-data selection; and (iii) an analysis of the learned allocations, which favour a small set of middle layers (10, 14--16, 18 and 20).

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{KV-Cache Optimization}

\textbf{Token eviction.} Eviction methods reduce KV-cache memory by retaining only the tokens judged important. H2O \citep{zhang2023h2o} keeps ``heavy hitters'', tokens with high accumulated attention, together with a window of recent tokens. SnapKV \citep{li2024snapkv} selects, for each attention head, the prompt positions that receive the most attention from an observation window at the end of the prompt. L2Norm \citep{devoto2024l2norm} keeps the tokens whose keys have the lowest $L_2$ norm, which correlates with high attention, without computing attention scores. All three give every layer the same budget.

\textbf{Budget allocation.} A second line of work redistributes a fixed total budget instead of changing how tokens are scored. Ada-KV \citep{feng2025adakv} allocates budget adaptively across the heads of a layer. PyramidKV \citep{cai2025pyramidkv} assigns layer budgets following a fixed pyramidal shape, larger in lower layers and smaller in higher ones. CAKE \citep{qin2025cake} derives per-layer budgets from layer preferences measured on attention patterns. Closest to our work, EvolKV \citep{yu2025evolkv} treats per-layer budgets as optimization variables, partitions layers into groups, and runs evolutionary search on one group at a time against downstream task score at one target budget; its main results then extend that allocation to larger budgets by proportional rescaling, without further optimization. MOSAIC shares this rescaling step; its differences from EvolKV are summarized in Section~\ref{sec:intro-evolkv} and Appendix~\ref{app:evolkv-glance}.

\subsection{Neural Architecture Search}

NAS has been successfully applied to model compression, hardware optimization, and neural network design \citep{elsken2018neural}. Multi-objective NAS balances competing goals (e.g., accuracy vs. latency) using Pareto-front optimization. MOSAIC adapts this machinery to the KV-cache allocation problem, treating per-layer budget as an architecture parameter.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Given an eviction method $M$ and target budget $B$ (average tokens per layer), we seek to find a vector $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, \ldots, b_{32}) \in [b_{\min}, b_{\max}]^{32}$ that maximizes task performance while maintaining $\frac{1}{32}\sum_{i=1}^{32} b_i = B$. This is a 32-dimensional constrained optimization problem. We use a per-layer floor $b_{\min} = 64$: SnapKV and H2O always retain a fixed 8-token recent window, so a layer at budget 16 would select only 8 tokens by attention, a degenerate regime that an earlier floor of 16 let the search exploit (up to 66\% of layers parked at the floor). Each configuration is encoded as $\mathbf{x} \in [0,1]^{32}$. The unconstrained search is multi-objective: it minimizes the mean per-layer budget $f_1(\mathbf{b}) = \frac{1}{32}\sum_i b_i$ and maximizes the task score $f_2(\mathbf{b})$ on a calibration subsample. The budget-constrained variant (Stage 4) instead fixes $f_1 = B$ by rescaling the per-layer weights so that their mean equals $B$ exactly.

\textbf{Search step.} All stages share one classifier-guided Bayesian optimization procedure. After each evaluation, the archive is non-dominated sorted, an MLP classifier (two hidden layers of 32 units) is trained to separate the rank-1 configurations from the rest, and differential evolution maximizes the classifier's probability of rank 1 over $[0,1]^{32}$ to propose the next configuration, one evaluation per iteration. We call these proposals BO proposals. The evaluation cap is 1000; in practice runs were stopped once the archive had settled.

\subsection{Search Procedure (MOSAIC — Four-Stage Pipeline)}

Searching every fixed budget directly is expensive and gives the optimizer no starting direction, so Stages 1--2 run one unconstrained search that yields a winner shape, Stage 3 tests how it translates to fixed budgets, and Stage 4 uses it to seed budget-specific refinement; the pipeline can stop after Stage 2 or 3.

\textbf{Stage 1 (Unconstrained Multi-Budget NAS):} Search a discrete per-layer grid with no budget constraint: seven levels $\{64,128,\dots,4096\}$ on RULER, for L2Norm and for H2O Code, and five levels $\{64,\dots,1024\}$ for the other LongBench runs (SnapKV, AdaKV, H2O). The initial design has 64 points: the uniform allocation at every grid level plus Latin Hypercube samples. Guided iterations then trace out the budget--score Pareto front. Because this search must rank allocations across the whole budget range, and runs only once per (task, method), it uses a larger calibration subsample on LongBench (30\%).

\textbf{Stage 2 (Winner Selection via Full-Data Eval):} Re-evaluate every configuration on the Stage 1 Pareto front (13--17 configurations per category for AdaKV) on the full test data, and keep the \textbf{winner shape}: the shaped configuration with the highest full-data score. Two exceptions, AdaKV on LongBench and H2O on RULER, took the best-\emph{calibration} configuration instead (see Limitations). This winner becomes a universal anchor for all subsequent budget-constrained searches. Full-data selection is essential rather than a formality. On LongBench, search-time calibration scores are optimistic (by 5.4 points for SnapKV and H2O and 1.1 for AdaKV, measured on the 10\% Stage 4 subsample) and rank candidates poorly: the calibration-best candidate is the true full-data best in only 5 of 23 SnapKV/H2O cells, 2 of 9 AdaKV cells and 10 of 16 L2Norm cells (mean Kendall $\tau \approx 0$, $0.47$ and $0.49$), 17 of 48 overall. On RULER, calibration is faithful for all four methods ($\tau = 0.77$--$1.00$; 18 of 19 correct picks). Table~\ref{tab:calib} summarizes this; per-cell values are in Appendix~\ref{app:calib}.

\textbf{Stage 4 (Budget-Constrained Adaptive Search):} For each target budget $B$, perform a dedicated BO search constrained to produce configurations with average budget exactly $B$. Seed this search with: (i) the Stage 2 winner rescaled to budget $B$ (from Stage 3), (ii) six heuristic shapes (uniform, ascending and descending ramps, middle- and edge-heavy triangles, and an alternating low/high pattern), and (iii) 57 Latin Hypercube (LHS) seed points, all drawn inside the constrained per-layer space (continuous weights rescaled to mean $B$). Guided BO iterations then extend this pool; run lengths varied from 97 to 1000 evaluations across methods and cells. Stage 4 evaluates many configurations for every budget, so it uses a smaller subsample (10\% on LongBench) to keep its per-budget cost comparable to EvolKV's; its top candidates are always re-evaluated on full data. RULER uses 10\% in both stages: its synthetic, near-binary subtasks already give faithful rankings at that size (Table~\ref{tab:calib}). The top calibration-scored configurations of this search are re-evaluated on full data, and the best of them is reported as \textbf{NAS-refined}. For each budget we thus report uniform, the rescaled Stage 2 winner (\textbf{Winner}), and NAS-refined; \textbf{MOSAIC's final configuration is the better of Winner and NAS-refined on full data} (Section~\ref{sec:which-stage}).

\textbf{Selection and evaluation data.} LongBench and RULER have no separate validation split, and the calibration subsets, the Stage 2 winner and the Stage 4 final configuration are all chosen using the benchmark examples on which we report scores. Every MOSAIC score is therefore measured on examples that influenced its selection. Stage 3 adds no per-budget selection, but the shape it rescales was selected on those examples: it measures how a selected shape transfers across budgets on the evaluated examples, not how it generalizes to unseen ones.

\subsection{Key Findings — LongBench}
\label{sec:which-stage}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the budget--score trade-off that the unconstrained search finds. Across all panels, 165 of 237 Stage 2 configurations lie above the uniform curve at the same average budget; the rest fall on or below it, which is why Stage 2 selects on full data. The winner that MOSAIC carries forward lies above the uniform curve in 13 of the 14 panels where uniform runs cover its budget. SnapKV's Code winner (58.26 at average budget 430) beats uniform at every budget up to 1024 (at most 56.73), and its Multi-Doc QA winner (36.14 at 274) beats uniform-1024 (35.87) with 27\% of its cache. The exception is AdaKV Single-Doc QA ($-0.40$), whose winner was selected on calibration score (Stage 2). Same-budget comparisons are in Table~\ref{tab:stage-best} and Appendix~\ref{app:cells}.

\textbf{Where NAS loses.} Across all evaluated cells, NAS beats uniform in 63 of 67 (SnapKV and H2O: 27/30; AdaKV: 14/15; L2Norm: 22/22), including every CODE, Summarization and RULER cell. The four losses are all LongBench QA and small: H2O MULTI\_DOC\_QA at B128 ($-0.41$), SnapKV MULTI\_DOC\_QA at B512 ($-0.04$), SnapKV SINGLE\_DOC\_QA at B256 ($-0.74$), and AdaKV SINGLE\_DOC\_QA at B512 ($-0.20$).

\textbf{Winner and NAS-refined.} Table~\ref{tab:stage-best} compares the two MOSAIC configurations with uniform allocation. The rescaled Stage 3 winner, which needs no search at the target budget, already beats uniform in 56 of 66 cells. The NAS-refined configuration beats uniform in 62 of 67 cells and matches or beats the winner in 47 of 66. Its extra gain is largest on RULER, where calibration scores are faithful (Stage 2): $+15.6$ against $+7.9$ for SnapKV and H2O, and $+23.5$ against $+5.5$ for L2Norm. On LongBench the two are close for SnapKV and H2O ($+0.69$ and $+0.59$), whose calibration scores rank candidates least reliably, and NAS-refined adds more for AdaKV and L2Norm. Per-cell results for all 67 cells are in Appendix~\ref{app:cells}.

\subsection{Key Findings — RULER}

RULER's needle-in-haystack tasks show much larger gains from per-layer optimization. Table~\ref{tab:ruler-summary} gives, for each method and budget, MOSAIC's final configuration: the best candidate in its pool on full data. For SnapKV at B64--B512 and AdaKV at B64, no Stage 4 search was run, so the entry is the rescaled Stage 3 winner. Every RULER cell improves on uniform, for all four methods:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SnapKV:} $+3.1$ to $+10.4$; at B1024 MOSAIC reaches 93.80 against 85.67 for uniform.
\item \textbf{H2O:} the largest gains, $+18.2$ to $+33.3$ at B1024--B2048; at B128 (floor 64) the gain is $+3.2$.
\item \textbf{AdaKV:} $+4.1$ to $+8.3$ from B128 to B2048.
\item \textbf{L2Norm:} $+2.3$ to $+47.9$, and $+31$ to $+48$ at B1024--B2048, all with floor 64.
\end{itemize}

These gains are substantially larger than on LongBench, reflecting RULER's structural task design: correct answers sit in specific positional windows. The gains concentrate on the hardest needle subtasks, where uniform allocation nearly fails. For SnapKV and H2O (floor-clean cells), NAS adds $+44.6$ on niah\_multikey\_3 and $+37.4$ on niah\_single\_3, versus $-0.6$, $+2.7$ and $+3.6$ on CWE, FWE and VT; for AdaKV the largest gains are also on niah\_multikey\_3 ($+49$ to $+82$ at B1024--B2048). L2Norm's uniform allocation fails most needle subtasks even at B1024, and MOSAIC recovers them (Appendix~\ref{app:ruler}).

\textbf{Caveat.} The RULER B1024 cells of SnapKV, H2O and AdaKV were searched under the earlier per-layer floor of 16; SnapKV and H2O use it (31\% and 66\% of layers sit at 16), and the floor-16 H2O B1024 configuration trades CWE accuracy (98$\rightarrow$22) for needle retrieval. B1536 and B2048 also ran with floor 16; for SnapKV and H2O their best configurations never reach it, while one AdaKV B1536 candidate (not the final configuration) does. AdaKV B64 also used floor 16. L2Norm was searched with floor 64 throughout, so its high-budget RULER gains are floor-clean.

\subsection{Shared Layer Structure}
\label{sec:shared}

Although each cell is searched independently, the resulting allocations share a common shape (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}). Across categories, budgets and the two methods analysed (SnapKV and H2O), six middle layers (10, 14--16, 18 and 20) receive more than their uniform share in 59--74\% of the 34 allocations (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}a), whereas early (0--9) and late (21--29) layers do so in only 15--50\% (median 28\% and 26\%). This structure is consistent with the rescaled Stage 3 winner transferring well across budgets; AdaKV shows a weaker version (Appendix~\ref{app:adakv-shapes}). The shared pattern is coarse, though, and allocations are task-specific: SnapKV's Code winner applied to Single-Doc QA falls below uniform at B128, B512 and B1024 ($-0.54$, $-1.08$, $-0.34$), and the Single-Doc QA winner applied to Code does too ($-0.81$, $-1.21$, $-0.63$). Shapes transfer better across eviction methods on the same task: H2O's Code winner used under SnapKV beats uniform ($+0.07$ to $+1.66$), and SnapKV's Code winner used under H2O does too ($+0.13$ to $+0.96$), but each usually trails the receiving method's own winner (the exception is H2O at B128, 46.75 vs.\ 46.59).

\subsection{Task and Method Patterns}

Gains are largest on RULER, intermediate on Code and smallest on LongBench QA; one untested hypothesis is that they track how localized the needed evidence is. Across methods, H2O shows the largest RULER gains ($+18$ to $+33$ at B1024--B2048). AdaKV, which already allocates budget adaptively across heads within a layer, still gains from reallocation across layers: $+4.1$ to $+8.3$ on RULER and up to $+1.98$ on LongBench, comparable to SnapKV. L2Norm shows the largest LongBench gains (Code $+4.9$ to $+9.5$), partly because its uniform baseline is weak: at B128 its Single-Doc QA score is 10.2, against 33.0 for SnapKV.

\section{Comparison with EvolKV}
\label{sec:evolkv}

We compare MOSAIC with a reproduction of EvolKV's core recipe \citep{yu2025evolkv}: the 32 layers are split into 4 groups of 8, and the 8 per-layer budgets of each group are optimized in turn with CMA-ES against a 30-sample calibration score, with earlier groups frozen, and the result is rescaled to the exact target budget. We run both of its settings: a separate search at each budget (\emph{direct}) and its main protocol, one search at budget 128 whose allocation is extended to larger budgets with EvolKV's proportional completion rule and no further optimization (\emph{expanded}). We use SnapKV on Code and Single-Doc QA and re-evaluate every configuration on the full test data with the same harness as Stage 4 (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\section{Conclusion}

On the evaluated benchmark examples, per-layer KV-cache budgets found by multi-objective search beat uniform allocation in 63 of 67 cells across four eviction methods, with large gains on structured retrieval (RULER) and small, occasionally negative, differences on LongBench QA. One unconstrained search transfers across budgets, and subsample scores do not reliably rank configurations on LongBench. The learned allocations favour a few middle layers and transfer across eviction methods, but not across tasks. Next: held-out evaluation, larger models and task-transferable allocations.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No independent held-out evaluation:} all MOSAIC scores come from examples that influenced configuration selection, so they show which explored allocations beat uniform on the evaluated examples, not how much of that advantage holds on unseen ones. Establishing this needs frozen configurations evaluated on unseen examples: newly generated RULER samples, and for LongBench a rerun of search and selection that excludes a held-out split.
\item \textbf{Search cost:} cost depends strongly on the eviction method. A SnapKV Stage 4 search costs about 8.5 A100 GPU-hours per cell at 200 evaluations, comparable to EvolKV; AdaKV evaluations are slower, and its Stage 4 searches cost 18--113 RTX A6000 GPU-hours each, about 1{,}000 GPU-hours for all AdaKV searches (Appendix~\ref{app:cost}). Stages 1--3 amortize across budgets, but budget-specific refinement does not. SnapKV LongBench Stage 4 run lengths (125--1000 evaluations) were set by a saturation watchdog rather than a fixed cap, while H2O runs are capped at 200. AdaKV searches were stopped by hand once the archive had settled or after a crash (97--359 evaluations; one reached 1000).
\item \textbf{Floor consistency:} RULER B1024--B2048 results (SnapKV, H2O, AdaKV), SnapKV RULER B64--B512 and AdaKV RULER B64 were searched under a per-layer floor of 16 rather than 64 (see the caveat before Table~\ref{tab:ruler-summary}); SnapKV RULER B64--B512 and AdaKV RULER B64 lack Stage 4 evaluations.
\item \textbf{Uneven settings across methods:} AdaKV's LongBench winners and H2O's RULER winner were selected on calibration rather than full-data score by an earlier version of the pipeline (for AdaKV the full-data-best configurations score 0.17--0.82 points higher, Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}; H2O's RULER front, winner at average budget 2180, was never re-evaluated on full data); stopping rules differed (manual, watchdog, or a 200-evaluation cap); AdaKV Summarization is still running.
\item \textbf{Benchmark specificity:} Gains are largest on RULER (structured retrieval) and smaller on LongBench (information-spread tasks). Applicability to other long-context domains (code synthesis, summarization) requires additional benchmarking.
\item \textbf{Model scale:} Tested on Llama-3-8B only. Layer-importance patterns may differ on larger models (70B+) or other architectures (Gemma, Mistral).
\item \textbf{Search components not isolated:} NAS-refined is the best configuration of the whole Stage 4 search, including its initial design (heuristic shapes and Latin Hypercube points); we do not separate the guided iterations from that design, which a random-search baseline at equal evaluation budget would do.
\item \textbf{Task and method dependency:} Allocations do not transfer across tasks (Section~\ref{sec:shared}), so each task needs its own Stage 1 search; cross-method transfer is measured only on Code.
\end{enumerate}
